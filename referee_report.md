## Summary

This article estimates the effect of U.S. minimum wage increases on low-wage employment by tracking how each increase reshapes the entire frequency distribution of hourly wages. The conceptual move is to replace a single aggregate employment regression with a bin-by-bin difference-in-differences event study over 138 prominent state-level increases (1979-2016), then read the overall employment effect off the bottom of the distribution as the sum of "missing jobs" just below the new minimum and "excess jobs" at or slightly above it. Because employment far above the minimum should not respond, the upper tail doubles as a built-in falsification test. This design lets the authors recover a meaningful first-stage wage effect even when only a small share of the workforce is directly affected, which the standard aggregate approach cannot do outside of subgroups like teens.

The main findings are that missing and excess jobs are close to equal, implying an affected-employment change of +2.8% (std. err. 2.9%) and a minimum-wage employment elasticity near zero, alongside a significant 6.8% wage gain of which roughly 40% is spillover. The paper extends this to demographic groups, sectors (with negative but imprecise tradeable-sector estimates), incumbents versus entrants, and the minimum-to-median (Kaitz) frontier, and it uses the distributional lens to argue that the large negative elasticities from two-way fixed-effects models load implausibly onto high-wage bins and are driven by pre-2000 confounding.

The contribution is genuine and the transparency of the wage-distribution decomposition is a real methodological advance. My main reservation is interpretive: several headline statements ("essentially unchanged," "some evidence of reduced employment in tradeable sectors") are affirmative readings of estimates whose standard errors are large enough that the design supports bounds on disemployment rather than point conclusions. The external-validity ceiling at 59% of the median also sits uneasily with the \$15 policies that motivate the paper.

## Strengths

- **Distributional decomposition turns the upper tail into a falsification test.** By estimating employment change in every \$0.25 bin, the design exposes exactly where any aggregate effect originates, and the flat, insignificant upper-tail bins (from \$5 above the minimum onward, individually and in the running sum) provide direct evidence that the identified effect is localized where the minimum wage can plausibly bite.
- **First-stage wage effect is strong and stable.** The 6.8% affected-wage gain is significant across all six robustness specifications (5.7-6.9% with common bin-period effects, 4.3-5.0% with division-specific effects), which anchors the employment elasticity in a genuine treatment rather than a weak instrument.
- **Diagnosis of the two-way-fixed-effects puzzle.** Decomposing the classic log-minimum-wage specification shows its negative aggregate elasticity accrues almost entirely in bins above \$15/hour and disappears in the post-1992 sample, a concrete and reproducible demonstration that the discrepancy with event-study estimates comes from pre-2000 confounding.
- **Spillover estimate separates real ripple effects from truncation artifacts.** Working with the frequency distribution rather than the wage density, and contrasting incumbents with entrants, lets the paper argue that measured spillovers are not an artifact of disemployment or of common measurement error.
- **Exact reproduction of the core tables and figures.** Independent re-execution reproduced Table I, the demographic and sector tables, and the main event-study figures essentially cell-for-cell, including the 138-event anchor values (Delta_a = 0.021, Delta_b = -0.018, bite 0.086/0.087).

## Weaknesses

- **The central "no job loss" conclusion is a non-rejection, not a demonstration of zero.** The affected-employment estimate (+2.8%, std. err. 2.9%) and the minimum-wage elasticity (0.024, std. err. 0.025) are equally consistent with modest job creation and small-to-moderate job loss. The abstract's "remained essentially unchanged" reads as an affirmative null the precision does not deliver; the defensible statement is an upper bound on disemployment.
- **The own-wage elasticity is uninformative about sign.** At 0.411 with a standard error of 0.430, the point estimate carries almost no information; only the one-sided lower bound (elasticities more negative than about -0.45 are ruled out) is usable, yet the positive point value is invoked as if it rejected the negative-elasticity literature on its own.
- **The abstract overstates the tradeable-sector result.** Tradeable and manufacturing employment effects (-11.1%, std. err. 13.6%; -10.1%, std. err. 14.5%) are insignificant, and the body correctly says so, but the abstract promotes them to "some evidence of reduced employment." A standard error two to three times the estimate does not support an affirmative claim.
- **Generalization stops at 59% of the median.** The identifying variation reaches only a Kaitz index of 59%, below what a \$15 floor implies in many states, so the flat employment-Kaitz relationship cannot speak to the policies that motivate the paper. This is conceded in the Discussion, but the abstract's "no evidence of disemployment when we consider higher levels" invites over-reading.
- **The measurement-error robustness argument rests on an untestable support condition.** Invariance of the net employment estimate to wage misreporting requires that workers misreported below the minimum truly come from [MW, W); mass truly above W misreported below MW would break the cancellation. The external administrative-data validation helps, but the load-bearing assumption should be stated as such.

## Reproducibility & code

The replication ported the authors' Stata pipeline to R/fixest (Stata being license-gated) and achieved a weighted replication score 0.98 (22 of 24 claims counted), with 21 of the 22 counted claims matching the paper and one partial. This is a strong result: the headline employment and wage estimates, the robustness columns, the demographic and sector tables, and the main event-study and two-way-fixed-effects figures all reproduced to printed precision.

- **Incumbent/new-entrant spillover contrast did not reproduce.** The overall and five demographic rows of the spillover table matched, but the re-run produced an incumbent wage effect near 0.067 (vs 0.095) and a new-entrant spillover share of about +0.07 rather than the reported -17.8%, flipping the sign. Because the incumbent-versus-entrant asymmetry is the empirical basis for the paper's within-firm relative-pay interpretation, this is the one substantive gap; it plausibly reflects placebo/far-above-bin controls not carried into the port, which indicates the documentation for that step is underspecified.
- **A text-quoted elasticity differs from the figure it describes.** The two-way-fixed-effects decomposition reproduced the figure's aggregate elasticity of -0.089 (std. err. 0.025) and its upper-tail concentration, but the in-text value of -0.137 (offered with "e.g.") is a different number that a reader cannot trace to the figure.
- **Event-by-event significance shares were not re-executed.** The distributional-significance statistics (5.3% of employment estimates significant, and the companion upper-tail and leading-effect shares) are appendix-level and fell outside the executed scope, so they remain unverified against re-execution.

## Recommended Changes

**Essential**

- Reframe the headline employment result (abstract "essentially unchanged," Discussion) as an upper bound on disemployment, stating the smallest negative elasticity the design can reject rather than asserting no effect, and present the own-wage elasticity of 0.411 as statistically indistinguishable from zero.
- Qualify the abstract's "some evidence of reduced employment in tradeable sectors" to match the body's "imprecise and statistically insignificant," and tie the "higher levels of minimum wages" claim to the observed 37-59% Kaitz range and where \$15 policies fall relative to it.
- Document the exact control set and bin range for the incumbent/new-entrant spillover decomposition so the -17.8% new-entrant share can be reproduced, since the interpretation of spillovers depends on it.
- Correct the Table III note to report 118 events (not 138), consistent with the table's event-count row and the 1992-2016 sector sample.

**Suggested**

- State the measurement-error support condition ([MW, W)) as an explicit assumption and bound how much true above-W mass misreported below the minimum would be needed to bias the net estimate.
- Reconcile the column (7) values in the text (0.025/0.029, 0.063/0.011) with Table I (0.027/0.028, 0.065/0.010).
- Adopt one consistent description of the excess-jobs upper edge (jobs up to \$5 above, matching k = 0..4) across the text, the W = MW + 4 definition, and the Figure III/Table I captions.
- Clarify which specification yields the in-text -0.137 versus the figure's -0.089.
- Fix the inverted spillover-share formula in the Table IV note, the negative-but-uninformative tradeable spillover description, the Figure IV bar/line color mismatch, and the conflicting Online Appendix D/E pointers for the sector classification.

## Follow-up studies

Two completed follow-up studies build directly on this replication. A "living update" rebuilt the analysis from raw microdata and extended it through 2024: it reproduces the paper era exactly (138 events; Delta_a/Delta_b/bite of 0.021/-0.018/0.087) and finds the wage gain holds in every era (+4.6%, std. err. 0.6%, pooled 1979-2024), but the central affected-employment estimate drifts from the paper's +2.8% (std. err. 2.9%, 1979-2016) toward roughly zero or slightly negative pooled through 2024 (-1.1%, std. err. 1.2%), with the most negative window the 2016-2019 \$15 ramp-up (-4.0%, std. err. 2.4%; marginal). This sharpens my external-validity weakness: the null holds in-sample but softens as the newer data cross the Kaitz frontier the paper flagged as untested (now reaching 0.67 versus the paper's 0.59). A second "theory update" argues the minimal revision consistent with both eras is a bunching model whose labor-demand elasticity declines with bite past a threshold near Kaitz ~0.50 (bite ~0.094): mild-monopsony-positive below, competitive-negative above, reproducing the paper era while resolving the 2016-2019 episode the flat model misses by 3.2 sigma. No cohort's job loss is yet statistically significant, so these results extend rather than overturn the paper, but they suggest the flat employment-Kaitz relationship is a sub-threshold feature rather than a universal one.

## Future Directions

- **Push the Kaitz frontier with post-2016 events.** The binned employment-Kaitz relationship is flat only to 59% of the median; the newer \$15-era events reaching 0.67 are exactly the range needed to test whether the frontier is truly flat or bends, as the follow-up theory model predicts.
- **Sharpen the tradeable-sector estimates with revenue or trade-exposure data.** The negative tradeable elasticities are the paper's most economically distinctive result but are too imprecise to interpret; linking to output/revenue measures (as in the Hungarian work the paper cites) could turn a suggestive contrast into an identified mechanism.
- **Exploit the incumbent/entrant distinction to test spillover theories directly.** Since the paper attributes spillovers to within-firm relative-pay norms rather than reservation wages, matched employer-employee data could adjudicate that mechanism where the CPS incumbent/entrant split can only hint at it.
- **Apply the upper-tail falsification diagnostic as a general model-selection tool.** The decomposition that exposes where the two-way-fixed-effects elasticity originates could be used to screen specifications in other place-based or price-floor policy settings.
